Every fall there is a week when we walk out of pickup and notice the light already going soft, and every spring there is a day when it suddenly feels like the whole evening is still ahead of us. We kept wondering how big those swings really are, so we built a small calculator to find out. Pick a city and the time school ends, and it works out how many minutes of daylight are left after the bell on any date, then draws the whole year so you can see the dark stretch coming and the bright one coming back.

    How we worked it out

    Sunset comes from the NOAA solar calculation, the same equations behind the NOAA Solar Calculator. It finds the sun's position for each date and solves for the moment its center sits 0.833 degrees below the horizon, which accounts for the bending of light near the horizon and the size of the sun. Civil twilight, the usable light, ends when the sun is 6 degrees below the horizon. We checked a handful of dates against another published implementation of the same math and they agreed to within a minute.

    Daylight after school is simply sunset minus the end time you picked, and it never goes below zero. We assume school ends at that same time every day of the year, so summer break and early release days are not taken out.

    The city list is about fifty of the largest US cities plus Redmond, each with its latitude, longitude, and time zone. Clock times use each city's own time zone through your browser, so daylight saving time starts and ends on the right dates, and places like Phoenix that skip it stay put. If you use your location, the coordinates stay in your browser and the time zone is your device's.
